1What the business has really generated: the history
The cash a business can hand to everyone who funds it (lenders and shareholders) is its free cash flow to the firm (FCFF). The textbook way to measure it starts from the income statement and builds it in five pieces, because each piece can then be forecast on its own: EBIT (operating profit before interest and tax), the tax on that profit (NOPAT = EBIT × (1 − tax rate) is what is left), plus D&A (a cost that did not use cash), minus CapEx (cash spent on equipment and buildings) and minus the increase in net working capital (ΔNWC) (cash tied up in inventory and receivables, net of what suppliers fund). The table below applies those steps to the last 10 fiscal years of SEC filings. The quick alternative is FCF = operating cash flow − capex, shown underneath as a cross-check: it can differ because operating cash flow also adds back things like stock-based pay. Look at several years, not one peak.
FCFF = EBIT × (1 − tax rate) + D&A − CapEx − ΔNWC quick check: FCF = Operating cash flow + Capex (capex is negative)

3Build free cash flow to the firm, year by year
With the curves set, each forecast year is built exactly like the history in step 1. Revenue grows by that year's growth rate; EBIT is revenue × the EBIT margin; tax is taken off EBIT to get NOPAT; D&A is added back (it was deducted in EBIT but cost no cash); CapEx and the increase in working capital are subtracted because they are cash that must be reinvested. What remains, FCFF, is the cash available to lenders and shareholders, and is what steps 4–6 discount. Edit a curve in step 2 and this table, and everything after it, updates.
FCFF(t) = EBIT(t) × (1 − tax(t)) + D&A(t) − CapEx(t) − ΔNWC(t) EBIT = Revenue × EBIT margin D&A, CapEx = % of revenue ΔNWC = % × (Revenue(t) − Revenue(t−1))
4The terminal stage: after the last forecast year
A company doesn't stop after the forecast, and we can't forecast for ever. So we assume its cash flow grows at a slow, steady rate (terminal growth, g) for ever after and use the shortcut for a growing perpetuity — the Gordon growth formula (for example $100 a year for ever at 10% is worth 100 ÷ 0.10 = $1,000; growing 3% a year it is worth 100 ÷ (0.10 − 0.03) = $1,429). Two rules: g must stay below the discount rate (WACC), or the value is infinite, and it should be near long-run economic growth (about 2–3%). Here the first terminal year is built from the year-10 drivers (revenue grows by g; margins, D&A %, CapEx % and ΔNWC % stay at their year-10 values), so the terminal cash flow keeps paying for the investment that growth needs. If year-10 CapEx is only as high as D&A, the model would assume growth for free: the check below shows the return on new capital that implies. The terminal value is often 50–80% of the total, so this input matters a lot.
Revenue(11) = Revenue(10) × (1 + g) FCFF(11) = EBIT(11) × (1 − tax) + D&A − CapEx − ΔNWC, all at year-10 ratios Terminal value = FCFF(11) ÷ (WACC − g) Implied return on new capital = g ÷ (reinvestment ÷ NOPAT)
5The discount rate: WACC
Money received later is worth less than money today, because you could earn a return elsewhere in the meantime: PV = Cash ÷ (1 + r)^n. The rate r we use is the company's WACC (weighted average cost of capital): the return investors demand for funding it — a blend of what shareholders demand (cost of equity) and what lenders charge (cost of debt), weighted by how much of the company each provides. A riskier company has a higher WACC, so its future cash is worth less today. Cost of equity uses beta (how much the stock moves with the market) and the equity risk premium (ERP), the extra return investors demand for stocks over risk-free Treasuries. Interest is tax-deductible, so debt's cost is reduced by the tax rate. FCFF belongs to lenders and shareholders, which is why it is discounted at WACC and not at the cost of equity alone.
Cost of equity = risk-free + beta × ERP After-tax cost of debt = cost of debt × (1 − tax)
Debt weight = Debt ÷ (Debt + Market cap) WACC = Equity weight × Cost of equity + Debt weight × After-tax cost of debt
One WACC is used for every year. Share price, beta and risk-free rate are market data (not in EDGAR): price and beta from yfinance, risk-free = latest 10-year Treasury yield; ERP is Damodaran's published figure; cost of debt = risk-free + 1 point (an assumption — use a real bond yield if you have one). Shares = latest cover-page count on the newest filing; debt = short-term + long-term debt from EDGAR (operating leases are not counted: their cost is already inside EBIT). 6From cash flows to value per share
Money received later is worth less than money today, so every forecast year's FCFF is discounted: PV = Cash ÷ (1 + WACC)^year. The discount factor (1 ÷ (1 + WACC)^year) is a number below 1 that shrinks each year. Add the discounted cash flows of all the forecast years, then add the discounted terminal value (it sits at the end of the last forecast year, so it is discounted from there). That total is the enterprise value (EV): the whole operating business. Shareholders own the business plus its cash, minus what it owes lenders (cash is not in the operating cash flows, and lenders are paid first), so add cash and subtract total debt (short- and long-term; using only long-term debt is a common slip). Divide by shares for the value per share. For young or distressed companies you can also weigh in the chance the business fails: value = going-concern value × (1 − p) + liquidation value × p.
Discount factor(t) = 1 ÷ (1 + WACC)^t PV(t) = FCFF(t) × Discount factor(t)
EV = Sum of PVs + PV of terminal value Equity value = EV + Cash − Total debt Value per share = Equity value ÷ Shares
Final value = DCF value × (1 − p) + Liquidation value × p (p = 0 by default)

7How sure are we? Sensitivity
A DCF gives a range, not one number: small changes to the discount rate or terminal growth move the value a lot, and the terminal value is the least certain part. The grid recalculates the value per share for five discount rates (rows, your WACC in the middle) against five terminal growth rates (columns, your growth in the middle). Cells are blue where the value is at or above today's price and red where it is below; your base case is outlined. A dash means the discount rate is not above growth, which makes no sense. The scenarios below use the same engine with three sets of assumptions: Bear (growth in years 1–5 −3 points, easing back to your curve by year 10; EBIT margin −2 points in every year; WACC +1 point), Base (your curves) and Bull (growth +3 points, EBIT margin +3 points, WACC −1 point). They shift your curves, so they stay meaningful after you reshape them.

8Reverse DCF: what is today's price already assuming?
Steps 1–7 asked what the business is worth given your assumptions. A reverse DCF flips that: it takes the market price as given and solves for the assumption the price implies. Start from the market enterprise value (market cap + debt − cash), keep the baseline cash flow, discount rate and terminal growth fixed, and solve for the revenue growth (or EBIT margin, terminal growth or discount rate) that makes the model's value equal the price. Then ask whether that is believable. If the price needs a margin the company has never earned, or growth far above anything in its history, you have found what the market is betting on. It is not a verdict: many combinations of growth and margin give the same price (see the trade-off table), and the answer depends on the rest of your curves.
Market EV = Price × Shares + Debt − Cash Solve for x such that DCF value per share (x) = Price (x = growth, margin, terminal growth or WACC, one at a time)
